01The short version

  • Pasco County is planning to extend Rangeland Boulevard 3.4 miles from Cattle Gap Trail to Bexley Village Drive, as a four-lane divided road with bike lanes, a sidewalk, a multi-use trail, and a new overpass across the Suncoast Parkway.
  • It is the first segment of a roughly 10-mile freight corridor in the county's long range plan, continuing east as Bud Bexley Parkway to US 41 and then as Caliente Boulevard to Ehren Cutoff.
  • The county's own 2050 forecast has Bud Bexley Parkway going from 2,100 vehicles a day to 16,500, and Bexley Village Drive rising sharply along every stretch.
  • Three build alternatives remain — A, D and E — all four lanes divided. Alternatives B and C were dropped after the public objected to a Suncoast Parkway interchange.
  • Design, right-of-way and construction money are already programmed in the county's capital plan for 2026, 2027 and 2029.

What is a freight corridor?

Pasco's long range transportation plan designates a Freight Priority Network — the roads the county plans around for goods movement. Rangeland Boulevard, Bud Bexley Parkway and Caliente Boulevard are all on it, and the plan draws its freight goals from the state's Florida Mobility and Trade Plan.

Being on that network isn't a rule about who may drive there. It means the road is intended to carry truck traffic, it is designed with that in mind, and the designation earns the project points in the plan's own project scoring. No truck volume forecast has been published for this corridor.

02Project overview

The study covers 3.4 miles between Cattle Gap Trail and Bexley Village Drive. The county's workshop mailer sets out two cross-sections, both four lanes divided — the difference is the width of the median, and therefore the total width of land the road occupies.

For a sense of width: 124 to 170 feet is the land a six- to eight-lane highway corridor typically occupies once medians, paths and drainage are counted. The driving surface is a smaller part of it than people expect.

03Where it is in the process

The project entered the county's adopted long range plan in December 2024. It is now in the route study stage, which is choosing between Alternatives A, D and E. After that come design, right-of-way purchase and construction — and money for all three is already set aside in the county's capital plan.

04How the Rangeland study fits into the larger Pasco plan

The workshop is about 3.4 miles. The adopted long range plan contains two more segments that continue straight on from it — same four lanes, same freight network designation, listed one after another.

This is not a standalone road. It is one piece of a network being built together.

05What the traffic forecast shows

What is AADT?

Average Annual Daily Traffic — the number of vehicles that pass a point on the road in a typical day, counting both directions together. It is the figure printed beside each street on the county's traffic boards.

Because it is an average across the whole year, it smooths out the differences between weekdays and weekends and between seasons. A street with an AADT of 16,500 carries more than that on a busy Thursday and less on a quiet Sunday. It also says nothing about the peak hours, which is when traffic is actually felt.

What is LOS?

Level of Service — a letter grade from A to F describing how much delay you experience at an intersection. A is free-flowing and each step down means a longer wait. F means the intersection cannot clear the vehicles arriving at it during the peak: the queue builds faster than it empties.

The county grades each intersection twice, once for the morning peak and once for the evening, and usually writes them together as one letter with the other in parentheses — so "D (F)" means D in the morning and F in the evening.

How the intersections change

Every intersection in the study area is graded before and after. Of the eleven that appear on both the 2023 and 2050 boards, none improves, one stays the same, and ten get worse.

Traffic on SR 54 itself falls slightly in the forecast. This is less a reduction in congestion than a redistribution of it — off the state road, onto the local network and the intersections.

06How these numbers are generated

Forecasts like these come from a computer model — the Tampa Bay Regional Planning Model. It works in four steps, and knowing them helps you read what the numbers can and can't tell you.

  • How many trips? The county divides itself into zones and estimates how many households and jobs each will hold in 2050. Households generate trips.
  • Where do they go? Trips get matched to destinations, with closer and easier ones more likely.
  • How do people travel? Mostly by car here.
  • Which roads? Trips are loaded onto the road network, and the model shuffles them until no one could get there faster by a different route.

Two things follow from that structure.

The number of households in 2050 is an input, not a result. It's the same in every alternative. So the model can show you how traffic redistributes when a road is added, but it can't show whether the road itself brings more development than was assumed.

The population forecast is on the high side. Florida planning normally uses the University of Florida's medium projection, which puts Pasco at about 815,800 people in 2050. The plan uses a county-produced figure of 1,018,698 — slightly above UF's high projection. The county explains it as a correction for a Census undercount plus its own growth modelling, and Pasco has genuinely grown fast for decades.

Pasco is growing and traffic will get worse

Pasco's population is forecast to roughly double between 2019 and 2050 — from about 562,000 to over a million people. Under any of these projections, low or high, traffic in our area gets substantially worse over the next 25 years whether or not Rangeland Boulevard is built.

That reframes what's actually being decided here. The choice isn't between more traffic and today's traffic. It's about where the additional traffic goes, which streets carry it, and what gets built to move it. Arguing that this road will bring more cars is true but incomplete; the harder question is what the alternative arrangement would be, and that's what the no-build comparison at Workshop #3 should show.

07What we still don't know

These are the gaps I couldn't close from public documents. Each is a fair question, and several may well be answered on September 24.

What does 2050 look like if the road isn't built?

Every published figure compares 2023 to 2050-with-the-road. That mixes 25 years of ordinary growth in with the project's own effect, so there's no way to tell which is which.

Status  The mailer says the no-build alternative will be presented at Workshop #3. This is the single most useful board there — read it first.

Is the Bud Bexley extension already in the traffic model?

If project 114.3 — Bud Bexley Parkway as a four-lane road to US 41 — is coded into the 2050 network behind these boards, then a large share of the 16,500 vehicles shown on our street comes from that project, not this one.

Why it matters  We'd be commenting on one project while looking at the combined effect of two.

How many trucks and how much noise?

Trucks. The corridor is on the county's Freight Priority Network — it is planned around goods movement. But the boards report total vehicles per day and never break out the truck share, and no truck forecast has been published for Rangeland Boulevard. Truck percentage is a routine output of traffic analysis, so the number exists somewhere; it just hasn't been shown.

Noise. Heavy trucks dominate road noise even when they are a small share of the traffic, which is why the truck percentage matters more to how a road sounds than the headline vehicle count does. A proper noise analysis — modelling future sound levels at nearby homes and testing whether barriers are warranted — is normally done at the design and environmental stage, after an alignment has been selected.

That sequence is the thing to ask about. The alignment is being chosen now, at this workshop. The study of what it will sound like comes afterwards.

Median width matters here too. Alternatives D and E use a 44-foot median instead of 22, which changes the overall width of the corridor and where the travel lanes sit relative to its edge — exactly the kind of difference a noise analysis would quantify. No comparison of the alternatives on noise has been published.

Worth asking  What truck percentage is assumed in the forecast, when will a noise analysis be carried out, and will residents see it before an alignment is locked in?

How different are Alternatives A, D and E for traffic?

The published traffic figures are Alternative A's. If median width or the pedestrian underpass changes what our streets carry, that hasn't been shown — and choosing between alternatives is exactly what this workshop is for.

How likely is the six-lane version?

The project overview notes that if Florida's Turnpike Enterprise builds a Rangeland Boulevard / Suncoast Parkway interchange, a six-lane segment would be required from South Branch Boulevard to west of Bexley Village Drive. No probability, cross-section or right-of-way width has been published for that case.

Why does this segment cost three times what the next one does?

The long range plan puts project 114.2 at $150.1 million over 3.41 miles — about $44 million a mile. The identical type of project immediately east of it, Bud Bexley Parkway, is costed at $14.8 million a mile in the same plan. The Suncoast overpass and land acquisition are plausible explanations, but no breakdown has been published.

Where are the other studies?

The project lists five goals: regional connectivity, easing congestion, evacuation and emergency response, secondary access, and safety. Only traffic has been shown publicly. Evacuation is measured with clearance times, safety with crash data, connectivity with detour distances — none of those has appeared.

What makes a comment count

Specific beats general. A question that names a document or a number has to be answered on the record; a statement of opposition gets logged as a statement of opposition. If you use anything from section 7, you can quote it directly.

It has worked before. Alternatives B and C both included a Suncoast Parkway interchange. The county's own mailer says they "are no longer being considered" because they "did not have sufficient public support to move forward." Whatever you think about this road, showing up demonstrably changes what gets built here.
