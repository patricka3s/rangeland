07  ·  What to do

Ten questions worth asking at the workshop

A question that names a document or a number has to be answered on the record. A statement of opposition gets logged as a statement of opposition.

  1. Where are the noise receptors, and what levels were modelled at each? Mitigation was found reasonable and feasible for one short segment near the Preserve. Everywhere else, which test did the location fail — reasonableness or feasibility — and by how much?
  2. What truck percentage does the 2050 forecast assume for the finished corridor? Existing counts are about 4% here and 12% on SR 54; the design-year number has not been published.
  3. Four of the five stated goals have no published measurement. Connectivity, congestion relief, evacuation, secondary access and safety. Which will be measured before an alternative is chosen, and how?
  4. Why has travel time never been measured on this corridor? The MPO's own congestion management process says speed, reliability and delay data are available through RITIS, using INRIX, HERE and NPMRDS. Two of the five goals are about mobility. Was that data ever run for these roads?
  5. The project takes about 10,000 vehicles a day off Bexley Village Drive. Which neighborhoods sit along the road that receives them, and have they been shown the same comparison?
  6. What are the updated construction estimates, and how much of the cost is the Suncoast Parkway and Anclote River bridges?
  7. Why does the adopted regional plan fund only a study in 2041–2050 while the county plans to build in 2029 — and has the state TRIP grant been awarded?
  8. Is the county pursuing the Suncoast interchange, and what counts as demonstrating stakeholder support? Has the Bud Bexley Pkwy / Bexley Village Dr intersection been tested with it through 2050?
  9. Does the noise result differ between A, D and E? The evaluation matrix only ever compared A and B, and B has been dropped.
  10. Can the no-build boards be shown beside the build boards? The county supplied the 2050 no-build figures in September 2026. Without them, none of the build numbers mean anything.

08  ·  Context

The question is not whether there will be more traffic. It is which streets carry it.

Rangeland Boulevard already runs west of the Suncoast Parkway. What is being decided is whether it is extended east and joined to Bud Bexley Parkway and Caliente Boulevard, making a continuous 10-mile corridor through Bexley.

Pasco's population is forecast to roughly double between 2019 and 2050. The county's own no-build figures show traffic here roughly tripling with nothing built at all, and the project moving a large share of it onto a road built to carry it. So the argument worth having is narrower than it looks: whether this alignment, at this width, on this timetable, is the right way to absorb growth that is coming regardless — and whether the effects have been measured before the choice is made.

01Key takeaways

  • Pasco county is growing. A lot. Traffic in our area is going to get worse, no matter what happens with the Rangeland study.
  • Pasco County is planning to extend Rangeland Boulevard 3.4 miles from Cattle Gap Trail to Bexley Village Drive, as a four-lane divided road with bike lanes, a sidewalk, a multi-use trail, and a new overpass across the Suncoast Parkway.
  • It is the first segment of a roughly 10-mile route on the county's Freight Priority Network in Pasco County's 2050 plan, continuing east as Bud Bexley Parkway to US 41 and then as Caliente Boulevard to Ehren Cutoff.
  • Traffic here roughly triples by 2050 whether or not this road is built. Bexley Village Drive by the roundabout goes from 6,100 vehicles a day to 23,500 in the county's no-build forecast. Growth, not the project, is doing most of that.
  • Compared with that no-build case, the project takes traffic off the streets we already live on — about 10,000 fewer vehicles a day on Bexley Village Drive toward SR 54 — and puts it onto the new road, which carries 18,000. Bud Bexley Parkway goes from 6,900 to 16,500.
  • Three build alternatives remain — A, D and E — all are four-lane divided roadways. (Alternatives B and C were dropped after strong public pushback.)
  • Design, right-of-way and construction money are already programmed in the county's capital plan for 2026, 2027 and 2029.

What is a Freight Priority Network?

Pasco's long range transportation plan designates a Freight Priority Network, the roads the county plans for the movement of goods. Rangeland Boulevard, Bud Bexley Parkway and Caliente Boulevard are all part of that plan, and the plan draws its freight goals from the state's Florida Mobility and Trade Plan. The county notes it has no freight plan of its own yet.

The designation earns the project a point in the 2050 plan's project scoring — the rule reads "project falls along the freight priority network — 1 point." The road is also designed for a WB-40 vehicle, a medium-sized semitrailer, meaning one can make the turns without leaving the pavement.

The county's view is that the designation does not imply heavy truck use, and that many state and county roads carry it. They report about 4% of daily traffic is trucks on the existing segments of this corridor against about 12% on the parallel stretch of SR 54, and expect the corridor's share to rise once the segments connect but stay below SR 54's — and above the local street network. A design-year truck percentage for the finished corridor still has not been published. See corrections 7 and 11.

02Project overview

The Rangeland study covers 3.4 miles between Cattle Gap Trail and Bexley Village Drive. The county's workshop mailer sets out two cross-sections, both four lanes divided — the difference is the width of the median, and therefore the total width of land the road occupies.

For a sense of width: 124 to 170 feet is the land a six- to eight-lane highway corridor typically occupies once you count medians, paths and drainage. The driving surface is a smaller part of it than most people expect.

One thing the drawings leave out, which the county pointed out and which I had misread: the cross-sections exclude the grassed strip between the sidewalks and shared-use paths and the right-of-way line. That strip is narrower in the wide-median section, which means the median can widen without the total right-of-way widening. A 44-foot median does not, by itself, mean the county takes more land. See correction 10.

The Suncoast interchange, and what would trigger six lanes

The project overview notes that if Florida's Turnpike Enterprise builds a Rangeland Boulevard interchange with the Suncoast Parkway, a six-lane segment would be required from South Branch Boulevard to west of Bexley Village Drive. The Turnpike ran a PD&E study from 2023 to mid-2025 that evaluated exactly that interchange.

Two organisations have said something about this, and they are answering different questions. Pasco County's position, in September 2026: "No interchange or six-lane section is currently being considered for approval in the Rangeland Blvd Route Study." The county adds that no probability of a future interchange or widening can be estimated, that the wider median is being considered only because it is a low-cost way to make later widening cheaper, and that whether any of that happens is for future county leaders to decide, whichever alternative is chosen.

Florida's Turnpike, which is the agency that would actually build the interchange, is more specific:

Advancement of the Rangeland Boulevard interchange to the design and construction phases has been suspended until Pasco County demonstrates stakeholder support and confirms that the Bud Bexley Parkway and Bexley Village Drive intersection will operate acceptably with the interchange through the year 2050.Greg Moore, Florida DOT Project Manager, via email

Two conditions sit in that sentence, and both are worth reading closely.

"Demonstrates stakeholder support." The interchange is neither cancelled nor approved. It is waiting on evidence of public support, and the county's route study is the process that would produce that evidence. This cuts in every direction: if you want the interchange, this is where you say so; if you don't, the same is true. Alternatives B and C were dropped from this study for lack of public support for a Suncoast interchange, so the mechanism is not hypothetical.

"Will operate acceptably … through the year 2050." The intersection named appears to be the Bexley Village Drive roundabout, which the county's published forecast already shows falling from A today to D in 2050 — and that is with the road built and no interchange. (Without the road at all it is forecast at F.) The Turnpike is asking the county to show the intersection still works with interchange traffic added on top of the build case. No such analysis has been published.

Worth asking at the workshop

Is the county pursuing the Suncoast interchange, and what would count as demonstrating stakeholder support? Has the Bud Bexley Parkway / Bexley Village Drive intersection been tested with the interchange in place through 2050, and can we see it? And if six lanes are triggered, how much wider does the corridor get — no cross-section or right-of-way width has been published for that case.

03Where we are in the overall process

This is an old project. It has been in the Pasco County Comprehensive Plan since 2006, and in long range transportation plans adopted by the county's MPO before 2024. It is now in the route study stage, which is choosing between Alternatives A, D and E. After that come design, right-of-way purchase and construction — and money for all three is already set aside in the county's capital plan.

Different studies happen at different stages, which matters if you want to know when a particular concern gets examined. Here is where each one lands.

I had this wrong, and it matters enough to say so plainly. An earlier version of this page argued that a step was missing — that a PD&E study normally sits between a route study and design, and that Pasco's funding request skipped it. The county's answer is that these are not two steps. A PD&E is the same process under federal rules; this is the local version, governed by Pasco County's code and standards. The environmental work I said was being deferred is happening inside the study that is running right now.

That has a consequence worth noticing. Because the county has chosen to follow the Federal Highway Administration's procedures for measuring and predicting highway traffic noise, the noise analysis for this corridor has already been done rather than postponed — and what it found is in section 6. The county keeps full discretion over what mitigation actually gets built.

Worth asking at the workshop

The project lists five goals: regional connectivity, easing congestion, evacuation and emergency response, secondary access, and safety. Traffic has been analysed and published, and environmental effects — noise, wetlands, species, contamination, historic sites — are screened in the alternatives matrix. But safety, evacuation and connectivity have no row in that matrix and no published analysis, even though safety is one of the five stated goals.

Evacuation is normally measured with clearance times, safety with crash data, connectivity with detour distances. How were the three alternatives compared on those, and can that comparison be published?

Four of the five goals have no published measurement of any kind. Congestion has been analysed and published. Connectivity, evacuation, secondary access and safety have not. Which of them will be measured before an alternative is chosen, and how?

05Traffic Increases No Matter What; the Build May Help

Key Term: AADT

Average Annual Daily Traffic — the number of vehicles that pass a point on the road in a typical day, counting both directions together. It is the figure printed beside each street on the county's traffic boards.

Because it is an average across the whole year, it smooths out the differences between weekdays and weekends and between seasons. A street with an AADT of 16,500 carries more than that on a busy Thursday and less on a quiet Sunday; more in the summer than in the winter. It also says nothing about the peak hours, which is when traffic is actually felt. That aspect is only included in the LOS ratings for intersections.

Until September 2026 this page could only show you two columns: what these streets carry today, and what they are forecast to carry in 2050 if the road is built. That comparison mixes twenty-five years of ordinary growth in with the project's own effect, and there was no way to separate them.

The county has now supplied the third column — the no-build forecast, which is what 2050 looks like if nothing is built at all. It changes the picture, and not in the direction I expected.

Traffic roughly triples on these streets either way. Bexley Village Drive by the roundabout goes from 6,100 today to 23,500 in 2050 with no project at all. Nearly all of the increase this page has been describing happens whether or not Rangeland Boulevard is extended. The county said as much in their response, and their own figure proves it.

And on the streets we already live on, the project takes traffic away rather than adding it. Bexley Village Drive toward SR 54 carries 32,500 a day in the no-build and 22,500 with the road built — ten thousand fewer vehicles. Ballantrae drops seven thousand. What the project does is move that traffic onto a new road built to carry it, which is the case for building it, and it is in the county's own numbers rather than in a brochure.

The trade is visible in the same table. Rangeland Boulevard carries 18,000 vehicles a day that are not there today, and Bud Bexley Parkway goes from 6,900 in the no-build to 16,500. So this is redistribution plus growth — not traffic conjured out of nothing, but not relief either. The honest question is not whether there will be more traffic. It is which streets carry it, and whether the people on the receiving end have been told.

Ten of eleven intersections get worse — with or without the road

Key Term: LOS

Level of Service — a letter grade from A to F describing how much delay you experience at an intersection. A is free-flowing and each step down means a longer wait. F means the intersection cannot clear the vehicles arriving at it during the peak: the queue builds faster than it empties.

The county grades each intersection twice, once for the morning peak and once for the evening, and usually writes them together as one letter with the other in parentheses — so "D (F)" means D in the morning and F in the evening.

Every intersection in the study area is graded with a Level of Service. Eleven appear in all three cases. Of those, ten are worse in 2050 than they are today — and that is true in the no-build as well as with the project. Only the Cattle Gap Trail roundabout holds its grade, and it holds it either way.

Set the two 2050 cases against each other and it reads differently. Alternative A is better than the no-build at five of the eleven, the same at six, and worse at none. Two of those five are large: Rangeland Boulevard at Gunn Highway is F in the evening peak if nothing is built and C if this is, and the Bexley Village Drive roundabout is F without the project and D with it.

Three intersections sit at grade F with the project. Six sit at F without it. Grade F means the intersection cannot clear the vehicles arriving during the peak — the queue builds faster than it empties — so the difference between three and six is not a rounding detail.

None of which makes the road a good idea by itself. It makes the case for it real, and it means the argument worth having is a narrower one: whether this particular alignment, at this width, on this timetable, is the right way to absorb growth that is coming regardless. The rest of this page is about the parts of that question the published documents still do not answer.

Worth asking at the workshop

Where does the traffic that leaves Bexley Village Drive actually go? Ten thousand vehicles a day come off that street in the build case and onto the new road. Which neighborhoods sit alongside the road that receives them, and have those residents been shown the same comparison?

And can we see the no-build boards for the intersections too? The county supplied the 2050 no-build traffic and level-of-service figures in September 2026, after I asked. They should be on a board at the workshop next to the build figures, because without them none of the build numbers mean anything.

06Trucks and noise

If this is a freight route, does that mean lots of trucks? Heavy trucks dominate road noise even when they are a small share of the traffic, so how a road sounds depends more on the truck percentage than on the total number of vehicles.

What is known about trucks

An earlier version of this page said no truck figures had been published for this corridor. The county has since supplied some. About 4% of daily traffic is trucks on the existing segments of the Rangeland Boulevard corridor, against about 12% on the parallel stretch of SR 54. They expect the corridor's share to rise once the segments are connected, but to stay below SR 54's — and, in their words, above the local roadway network. The road is designed for a WB-40 vehicle, a medium-sized semitrailer.

What still has not been published is a number: the truck percentage the 2050 forecast actually assumes for the finished corridor. Existing counts on a disconnected road are not the same thing as a design-year assumption for a connected one, and the noise analysis depends on it. That is the question worth asking, and it is narrower than the one this page used to ask.

The noise analysis has been done — and this page had it wrong

The route study's alternatives evaluation matrix scores "potential impacts to noise conditions" as None for the no-build option and High for both build alternatives it compared. That is a coarse screening judgement, published in January 2025, and an earlier version of this page treated it as the only noise work that had been done — and argued that the real analysis had been deferred to a study nobody had funded. That was wrong. Correction 6 has the detail.

The county explains the High rating simply: no roadway exists there today, so any road raises noise levels along the whole corridor from a baseline of essentially nothing. And the detailed work has already happened. The county has chosen to follow the Federal Highway Administration's procedures for measuring and predicting highway traffic noise, and under those procedures:

noise mitigation measures were only found to be reasonable and feasible for one short segment of the project near the Preserve community.Pasco County Transportation Engineering Department, September 2026

That is a much more specific statement than "High," and it cuts two ways. It means a barrier is on the table for one stretch of the corridor. It also means that for every other stretch — the rest of 3.4 miles of new four-lane road — the analysis concluded that mitigation was either not reasonable or not feasible. Under the FHWA process those are defined tests involving cost per benefited home, how much noise a barrier would actually remove, and whether one can be built at all.

Nobody outside the county has seen the underlying numbers: which homes were modelled as receptors, what sound levels they were predicted to experience, and which test each location failed. That is the gap now, and it is a narrower and more answerable one than the gap this page claimed before.

One caveat carries over. The matrix compared No-Build against Build A and Build B. Build B has since been dropped, and the alternatives now on the table are A, D and E. Whether the noise result differs between those three has not been published.

Worth asking at the workshop

Where are the noise receptors, and what were the modelled levels at each? Mitigation was found reasonable and feasible for one short segment near the Preserve. For everywhere else, which test did the location fail — reasonableness or feasibility — and by how much?

What truck percentage does the 2050 forecast assume for the completed corridor? And does the noise result differ between A, D and E, given that the matrix only ever compared A and B?

How it compares to the rest of the plan

The long range plan gives a total cost for every project, and its Appendix B gives a length for each one, so it was easy enough to work out a cost per mile. Doing that for all 40 road projects puts this segment eighth from the top — and well above the segment that continues straight on from it.

The ranking is not the interesting part. Every project above it on that list is short — between 0.8 and 1.7 miles. At 3.41 miles, Rangeland is twice the length of anything else in the top eight. That matters because short projects are expensive per mile for an obvious reason: a bridge or an interchange costs what it costs, and dividing it over one mile gives a bigger number than dividing it over three. A long project carrying a structure should come out cheaper per mile, not dearer.

A gap that size is not automatically a problem, and the county says the comparison is misleading:

The per mile cost comparison to the area-wide averages are misleading because this project includes an entirely new roadway with new bridges spanning the Suncoast Parkway and the Anclote River (along with one alternative that also includes an additional pedestrian underpass).Pasco County Transportation Engineering Department, September 2026

That is a disagreement about what the number means rather than a claim that the arithmetic is wrong, so the figure stays and their objection sits beside it. It is a fair point as far as it goes: structures are expensive and a road carrying two of them should cost more per mile than one carrying none.

What I would still want to know is whether the projects it is being compared against carry structures of their own — the comparison set is 40 road projects from the same plan, and several of them cross water or rail. I am working through Appendix B to find out, and I will publish the answer either way. The county also says the construction estimates are being updated and will be presented at the workshop, so the figures above may be superseded on 24 September. Ask for the new ones.

What is actually funded, and by whom

Two documents describe the money for this road, and they do not describe the same project.

The regional plan funds a study, in the 2040s. In the 2050 plan's cost-feasible table — the list of what the region expects to be able to afford — project 114.2 receives $2,788,496, all of it for a PD&E study, in the 2041–2050 band. The design, right-of-way and construction columns are blank. The $150.1 million above comes from a different table, the needs list, which prices projects whether or not the money exists.

The county is moving two decades sooner, with its own money. The county's capital plan funds design in 2026, right-of-way in 2027 and construction in 2029. The MPO's priority list has Rangeland at number 10 under "construction unfunded," with the funding request marked TRIP — the state's Transportation Regional Incentive Program, which the plan's own fund-code table describes as 100% state money, and which requires the county to match it dollar for dollar.

Which means no federal highway money is committed to this road. It is county capital plus a state grant request that has not yet been awarded. That is worth knowing for two reasons: federal funding normally brings a federal environmental review, and a project whose construction money depends on a pending state grant is less settled than the capital plan makes it look.

Worth asking at the workshop

How much of the $93 million construction cost is the Suncoast Parkway overpass? Why does this segment cost roughly three times per mile what the adjoining segment costs? And does the $150.1 million in the long range plan represent the same scope as the $111.8 million in the route study, just in later-year dollars?

Then the bigger one: why does the adopted regional plan fund only a study in 2041–2050 while the county plans to build in 2029, and has the TRIP grant actually been awarded?

08How the county comes up with its numbers

The traffic forecasts in section 5 come from a computer model — the Tampa Bay Regional Planning Model. It was developed by the region's metropolitan planning organizations with the Florida Department of Transportation, its results are put through a validation process, and it is updated as plans change. It is not a Pasco County invention and it is not unusual; every metropolitan area in the country forecasts traffic this way.

It works in four steps, and knowing them helps you understand what the data can and can't tell you.

  • How many trips? The county divides itself into zones and estimates how many households and jobs each will hold in 2050. Households generate trips.
  • Where do they go? Trips get matched to destinations, with closer and easier ones more likely.
  • How do people travel? Mostly by car here.
  • Which roads? Trips are loaded onto the road network, and the model shuffles them until no one could get there faster by a different route.

Two things follow from that structure.

The estimated number of households in 2050 is likely the primary input. The number of new houses drives the amount of traffic. The model can show you how traffic redistributes when a road is added, but it can't show whether the road itself brings more development than was assumed, which would in turn increase traffic.

The population forecast used by Pasco is on the high side. Florida planning normally uses the University of Florida's medium projection, which puts Pasco at about 815,800 people in 2050. The plan uses a county-produced figure of 1,018,698 — slightly above UF's high projection. The county explains it as a correction for a Census undercount plus its own growth modelling, and Pasco has genuinely grown fast for decades.

What the model doesn't answer

The model does one job — forecasting how many vehicles end up on each road — and it is does that reasonably well. But several of the project's five stated goals are outside what it can measure. On top of the two limits above, here is what it never addresses:

  • Whether new capacity generates new driving. How many trips a household makes is set by what kind of household it is, not by how easy driving has become. Add a road and the model will move trips onto it and shift where some of them go — but the total number of trips does not change. That is exactly the mechanism the induced-demand research argues is real, and the model has no way to represent it.
  • How long the rush hour lasts. The output is a daily total, converted to a peak-hour grade using standard factors. It says nothing about whether the one bad hour becomes two or three bad hours, which is how congestion is usually experienced.
  • Anything about crashes. A travel demand model produces volumes. Predicting safety outcomes takes different methods and actual crash data, done as separate work. Safety is one of the five goals.
  • Anything about noise or air quality. Those take traffic volumes as an input and run them through separate models. The noise rating in section 6 came from a screening checklist, not from a detailed analysis.
  • Evacuation performance. Clearance times assume a hurricane — it again requires a different model entirely. Evacuation is also one of the five goals.
  • What happens on the smallest streets. A regional model codes arterials and collectors. Streets below that size are often not in the network at all, so cut-through routing inside a neighborhood is finer than the tool can resolve.
  • How uncertain a 2050 number is. Every figure is published as a single number, as if it were absolute. Any projection 25 years out will have a wide margin of error.

The boards only measure congestion. Safety, evacuation, noise, pollution, truck volume... all of these are unaddressed.

Pasco is growing and traffic will get worse

Pasco's population is forecast to roughly double between 2019 and 2050 — from about 562,000 to over a million people. Under any of these projections, low or high, traffic in our area gets substantially worse over the next 25 years whether or not Rangeland Boulevard is built.

So the choice isn't between more or less traffic. It's about where the additional traffic goes, which streets carry it, and what gets built to move it. Arguing that this road will bring more cars is true but incomplete; the harder question is what the alternative arrangement would be, and that's what the no-build comparison at Workshop #3 should show.

Worth asking at the workshop

The 2050 household and job totals are an input to the model, identical in every alternative, and the population figure behind them sits above the University of Florida's high projection. What would these forecasts look like using UF's medium projection instead, and how sensitive are the volumes on our streets to that choice?
